How to Improve Ecommerce Conversion Rates: A Practical Guide

By the Brainiac Marketing Agency team · November 2, 2026 · 9 min read

Your conversion rate is the share of visits that end in an order. Improving it means more sales from the traffic you already pay for or work to attract. It doesn't come from a single trick; it comes from finding where shoppers struggle and removing those obstacles one by one.

Measure before you change anything

Average conversion rates vary widely by industry, price point, device and traffic source, so comparing yourself with a general benchmark is rarely useful. Compare your store with itself instead:

  • Overall conversion rate, by device and by traffic source
  • Product page to add-to-cart rate
  • Cart to checkout rate
  • Checkout to order rate

Make sure purchase tracking is accurate before you rely on the numbers. Place a test order and check it appears once, with the right value, in your analytics.

Fix the checkout first

Every order passes through checkout, so problems there affect everything. Research by the Baymard Institute puts the average documented cart abandonment rate at about 70%, and its survey of US shoppers points to consistent causes: unexpected extra costs, required account creation, a long or complicated checkout, and not trusting the site with card details.

  • Show delivery costs, or the free-delivery threshold, before checkout.
  • Allow guest checkout.
  • Remove optional form fields and enable address autocomplete if your platform offers it.
  • Offer the express payment options your customers use.
  • Make the returns policy easy to find from the cart.

Make product pages do more of the selling

Shoppers can't touch the product, so the page has to answer their questions. The most effective improvements are usually simple: a clear first photo, the price and key benefits visible without scrolling, sizing and delivery information next to the Add to cart button, and genuine reviews. Our guide on product pages that help customers buy goes through each element.

Help shoppers find the right product

  • Name menu items the way customers describe products, not by internal categories.
  • Make on-site search forgiving of typos and synonyms, and check what people search for.
  • Add filters that match real buying decisions, such as size, material or price.
  • Make sure every collection page shows products quickly, especially on mobile.

Build trust for first-time visitors

New visitors don't know your brand. Show who you are, how to contact you, delivery times, returns and real customer feedback. Keep design consistent and fix anything that looks broken. Avoid pressure tactics such as fake countdown timers or invented stock warnings; they can work against trust and may break consumer protection rules in some countries.

Give mobile shoppers their own review

Check your best-selling product, the cart and the checkout on a real phone. Look for buttons that are hard to tap, pop-ups that cover content, slow-loading images and steps that need zooming. If your mobile conversion rate is much lower than desktop, this is usually where to start.

Test changes properly

A/B testing compares two versions of a page with real visitors. It's useful, but it needs enough traffic and orders to produce a reliable answer. With lower traffic, focus on clear usability fixes and measure before-and-after over a few weeks, taking seasonality and promotions into account.

Keep a simple log of every change with the date, so you can connect changes in your numbers to what you did.

Bring back visitors who weren't ready

Many visitors are researching, not buying today. Welcome emails for new subscribers and abandoned cart reminders bring interested people back when they're ready. Our ecommerce email marketing service sets up these flows.

A simple monthly routine

  1. Review the funnel by device and source.
  2. Pick the biggest drop-off and list possible causes.
  3. Implement two or three focused changes.
  4. Record the changes and measure for a few weeks.
  5. Keep what helped, revert what didn't, and repeat.
